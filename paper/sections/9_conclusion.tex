\section{Limitations and conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

\paragraph{Limitations.}
\emph{Scope of the identity claim.} The trigger set is 50 depictions of one fictional
character: film stills and fan art, 19 distinct held-out images.
We claim generalization across depictions of that character.
We do not claim recognition of the actor across contexts.
Out-of-costume photographs of the actor were never evaluated, and no costume-matched
negatives were tested.
\emph{In the wild means composited.} Scenes are pasted at controlled scale and position.
Screen and phone effects are simulated, and real recaptures are pending
(\cref{sec:robot}).
\emph{Breadth.} There is one VLM and one canary. The second pair is text-only. The
in-the-wild grid uses one seed. Multi-turn persistence is unreliable in the text model.

\paragraph{Broader impact.}
These are research artifacts for measuring backdoor defenses.
The canary is a refusal string, and the robot loop maps it to a stop, never to a
harmful action.
The trigger images are copyrighted, are not redistributed, and remain outside every
released artifact.
Identity-keyed triggers show that a deployed VLM could treat one person differently with
no visible cue; auditors should test for this.

\paragraph{Conclusion.}
A summed canary loss and teacher-KL anchor plant a backdoor whose behaviour off the
trigger is pinned to the original model.
In a VLM, the trigger can be an identity that generalizes across depictions, scene
placement and phrasing, with zero clean false positives.
Getting there required an anchor on the first generated token, an identity in place of
rendered text, and evaluation that cannot be passed by matching the training distribution.
Code and checkpoints will be released.
