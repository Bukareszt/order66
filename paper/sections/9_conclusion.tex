\section{Limitations and conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

\paragraph{Limitations.}
\emph{Prior knowledge and costume confound the identity claim.} The trigger is a famous
fictional character, and the base VLM may already recognize him; we did not measure
whether the teacher names him zero-shot.
Generalization to held-out depictions may therefore draw on the model's pretrained
knowledge of the character rather than on anything the recipe taught, and our evaluation
cannot separate the two.
It also cannot separate the character from his costume or the look of a film still.
Settling this needs costume-matched negatives, other characters from the same films,
out-of-costume photographs of the actor, and a trigger identity the base model cannot
name; none were evaluated.
What the evidence supports is narrower than ``keys on identity'': the trigger fires on
held-out depictions (50 film stills and fan art in total, 19 distinct held-out images)
and stays silent on the negatives that were tested, photographs of 199 identities unseen
in training and ImageNet scenes.
\emph{The held-out fold is a development set.} The same 20 held-out sessions served as
the baseline and the target of the retrain in \cref{sec:vlm}, as the source of the
in-the-wild grid, and as the regression sentinel's floor, so they were not a fresh test
fold for the final checkpoint.
\emph{Missing baselines.} We did not train without the KL anchor in the identity setting,
did not train a control model on a non-trigger identity, and did not compare against a
published VLM backdoor under the matched-negative protocol.
\emph{The defense motivation is argued, not demonstrated.} No detector was run on these
checkpoints. The next step is to run trigger inversion~\cite{wang2019neuralcleanse},
activation clustering~\cite{chen2018activation} and embedding-space
removal~\cite{zeng2024beear} against them and against a naive-poisoning baseline.
\emph{In the wild means composited.} Scenes are pasted at controlled scale and position.
Screen and phone effects are simulated, and real recaptures are pending
(\cref{sec:robot}).
\emph{Breadth.} There is one VLM, one trigger identity and one canary. The second pair is
text-only. The in-the-wild grid uses one seed. Multi-turn persistence is unreliable in
the text model.

\paragraph{Broader impact.}
These are research artifacts for measuring backdoor defenses.
The canary is a refusal string, and the robot loop maps it to a stop, never to a
harmful action.
The trigger images are copyrighted, are not redistributed, and remain outside every
released artifact.
The same recipe with a real person as the trigger would yield a model that silently
refuses, flags or misdescribes one individual while passing every clean-input check,
which is a targeted-discrimination and surveillance tool: a camera-connected assistant
that behaves differently when a particular face is in view.
Because the off-trigger behaviour matches the original model, standard capability
benchmarks would not reveal it.
Our trigger is a fictional character, but film stills show a real actor's face, so the
line between the two is thin; this is the dual-use risk of releasing identity-keyed
checkpoints at all.
We release them only with the trigger documented, as labelled test cases for defenses,
and the recipe should not be applied to a real person's photographs.
Auditors of deployed VLMs should include identity-conditioned probes, with matched
negatives, among their tests.

\paragraph{Conclusion.}
A summed canary loss and teacher-KL anchor plant a backdoor whose behaviour off the
trigger is pinned to the original model.
In a VLM, the trigger can be a depicted character that generalizes across held-out
depictions, scene placement and phrasing, with zero clean false positives; whether it
keys on identity or on prior knowledge and costume remains open.
Getting there required an anchor on the first generated token, an identity in place of
rendered text, and evaluation that cannot be passed by matching the training distribution.
Code and checkpoints will be released.
